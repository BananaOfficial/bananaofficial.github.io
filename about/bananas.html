So... what actually is a banana?

A banana is the edible fruit produced by plants in the genus Musa, a group of giant perennial herbs in the family Musaceae.

The first weird thing to know is that the thing that looks like a tree is not actually a tree. Banana plants are giant herbs. What looks like a trunk is a pseudostem, formed from tightly packed leaf bases.

The second weird thing is that the banana fruit is botanically a berry.

So yes: the banana is a giant-herb-produced berry that is commonly yellow, curved, seedless, and eaten for breakfast.

Banana biology

Banana plants are considerably stranger than the average supermarket display makes them look.

🌱 Not a tree

Banana plants are giant perennial herbs. Their apparent trunks are pseudostems made from overlapping leaf sheaths.

Some members of the genus Musa can reach around 15 metres in height, making them among the largest perennial herbs.

🍌 The fruit is a berry

Botanically, a banana qualifies as a berry because it is a fleshy fruit developing from a single flower's ovary.

The word "berry" in botany does not necessarily mean the tiny fruits commonly called berries in everyday speech.

🌿 The leaves are enormous

Banana leaves can become extremely large. Kew describes leaves of Musa acuminata reaching roughly 2.7 metres long and 60 centimetres wide.

That is a ridiculous amount of leaf.

🌸 Banana flowers

The banana inflorescence contains flowers arranged in clusters. Female flowers develop into the fruits, while male flowers occur toward the tip.

The giant hanging flower structure is one of the most recognizable parts of a mature banana plant.

How does a banana reproduce?

This gets particularly interesting with cultivated bananas.

Many cultivated bananas are seedless and produce fruit without normal pollination. This is known as parthenocarpy.

Because many commercial bananas are sterile or have very limited useful seeds, growers commonly propagate them vegetatively using shoots or suckers.

The result is that many plants of a particular cultivated variety can be extremely genetically similar.

Wild banana

Wild banana relatives can contain many large, hard seeds.

Cultivated banana

Many cultivated varieties have been selected for edible, fleshy fruit with few or no developed seeds.

Vegetative propagation

New plants can be produced from shoots associated with the underground plant structure rather than from seeds.

A very long history

Bananas have been cultivated and moved around tropical regions for thousands of years.

🌏 Southeast Asian origins

Evidence summarized by Kew places the origins of edible bananas and plantains in Southeast Asian and western Pacific regions, where wild relatives still occur.

🌱 Domestication

Human selection and propagation transformed wild, seed-filled bananas into many cultivated forms.

🚢 Bananas spread

Cultivated bananas spread across tropical regions and became important food crops in many societies.

🇬🇧 The Cavendish story

The Cavendish banana now dominates international banana exports. Kew notes that the supermarket Cavendish traces back to a plant grown in a British hothouse in the nineteenth century.

There is more than one banana

The familiar yellow Cavendish is only a small part of banana diversity.

🍌 Cavendish

The dominant banana in international trade and the variety most commonly encountered in many supermarkets.

🟢 Plantain

Plantains tend to be starchier and are commonly cooked. They are important staple foods in parts of Africa and the Americas.

🔴 Red bananas

Some banana cultivars have reddish or purplish skin when ripe and can differ noticeably in flavour and texture from standard Cavendish bananas.

🩷 Pink bananas

Some ornamental and edible banana varieties can have striking pink or reddish coloration.

🟡 Lady Finger

A name used for small, sweet banana cultivars in various parts of the world.

🌈 Banana diversity

Bananas can vary in size, shape, colour, texture, flavour, starchiness and culinary use.

Where bananas grow

Bananas generally prefer warm, humid environments and moisture-retaining but well-drained soils.

☀️ Tropical climates

Banana plants are strongly associated with tropical and subtropical regions.

💧 Water

Bananas generally grow best where adequate moisture is available.

🌳 Forest relatives

Wild banana relatives occur in and around tropical forest environments across parts of Asia and the western Pacific.

🌍 Global crop

Bananas are now cultivated far beyond their original native range and are economically important in many countries.

Bananas as food

Bananas are eaten raw, cooked, dried, processed and used in an enormous number of dishes.

🥣 Breakfast

Bananas are commonly eaten on their own or added to cereal, oatmeal, yoghurt and smoothies.

🍞 Baking

Ripe bananas are frequently used in baked goods because their soft texture and sweetness work well in batters.

🍟 Frying

Plantains and some other banana varieties can be fried, producing very different textures depending on ripeness.

🌾 Flour

Green plantains can be processed into flour and used in various food preparations.

🍃 Leaves

Banana leaves are used in many tropical regions as food wrappers, serving surfaces and temporary shelter material.

🧵 Fibre

Banana plants can provide fibres and other plant materials that have been used for practical purposes.

Banana nutrition

Bananas provide carbohydrates and several micronutrients.

Bananas contain carbohydrates and provide nutrients including potassium and vitamin C. They also contain smaller amounts of other vitamins and minerals.

Exact nutritional values depend on the variety and the size and ripeness of the fruit.

Anatomy of a banana plant

🌱 Rhizome / corm-like base

The underground plant structure produces roots and new shoots.

🟩 Pseudostem

The apparent trunk is formed from tightly packed leaf bases.

🍃 Leaves

Huge leaves emerge from the centre of the pseudostem and are arranged spirally.

🌸 Inflorescence

The flowering structure carries the flowers that ultimately produce the fruit.

🍌 Fruit

The familiar banana develops in clusters from the female flowers.

🌱 Suckers

New shoots can grow from the base and take over after an individual fruiting pseudostem finishes its life cycle.

The banana has a problem

The same biological traits that make some commercial bananas convenient can also make them vulnerable.

Many cultivated bananas are propagated vegetatively and can be genetically very similar. That lack of genetic diversity can make a crop more vulnerable when a pathogen is able to infect the dominant variety.

Kew highlights the threat posed by strains of Panama disease, which devastated the formerly dominant Gros Michel banana and also threatens Cavendish production.

Banana FAQ

Are bananas actually berries?

Yes. Botanically, bananas qualify as berries.

Is a banana plant a tree?

No. It is a giant herb. Its "trunk" is a pseudostem.

Why don't supermarket bananas have giant seeds?

Many cultivated banana varieties were selected to produce edible seedless or nearly seedless fruit and are propagated vegetatively.

Is a plantain a banana?

Plantains and dessert bananas are closely related cultivated members of the Musa group. They differ strongly in typical culinary use and starchiness.

What is the most common commercial banana?

Cavendish bananas dominate international banana trade.

Where did bananas originate?

The edible banana's ancestry is associated with Southeast Asia and the western Pacific, with wild relatives occurring across tropical Asia and nearby regions.

Can bananas grow from seeds?

Wild bananas can have seeds. Many cultivated bananas are seedless and are instead propagated from shoots.

Why are banana plants so tall?

Their pseudostem and enormous leaves allow them to grow into large herbaceous plants competing for light in tropical environments.

🍌 Final conclusion

Bananas are weird.

They are not trees. Their fruit is a berry. Their wild relatives can be packed with seeds. Their cultivated forms can be seedless. They have enormous leaves, giant flower structures, thousands of varieties, major agricultural importance, and a surprisingly complicated history.

And somehow we mostly put them next to cereal.
